Introduction

“God has given to man no sharper spur to victory than contempt of death.” - Hannibal

Hannibal Barca of Carthage (247 BC - 183-181 BC) is one of the most interesting historical figures of all time, at least to me. As an amateur historian, I’ve studied Hannibal’s campaigns in the Second Punic War for many years now. Not to brag, but this is one of the few topics that I do consider myself an expert in. I first learned about the Legend of Hannibal from one of my teachers in either 7th or 8th grade (I still vividly remember the exact moment when he talked to us about this). I’ve never forgotten about Hannibal’s story because it’s a story that’s stranger than fiction, but it’s one of the best underdog stories that’s ever been recorded in human history. I’ve personally: read one book, watched one movie, watched multiple documentaries, and read multiple articles on Hannibal. He’s a big inspiration to me and a historical figure that has given me enormous strength over the years. Let me summarize everything that I’ve learned over the course of at least 15+ years.

Background Context

Before we get to Hannibal, I have to set the context of who the Carthaginians were and the state of the world Hannibal was born into. Who were the Carthaginians that we hear mentioned in obscure history books or quickly referenced in movies? The Carthaginian Republic (814 BC - 146 BC) was an ancient civilization that occupied the coastal lands of modern-day North Africa, with the heart of the republic located in modern-day Tunisia. The republic started expanding out of the city of Carthage (near modern-day Tunis, Tunisia) and dominated the coastal lands of North Africa and modern-day southern Spain, with dreams of becoming a great power. The Carthaginians were a people known especially for their strong maritime trade/commerce and naval power. The Carthaginians were also a proud people who envisioned having total hegemony over the Mediterranean Basin, the region of land and sea surrounding the Mediterranean Sea. But as fate would have it, they had a rival based in modern-day Italy that also had the same idea: the Roman Republic (509 BC - 27 BC; note that these are the dates of the Roman Republic, not the Roman Empire). The Roman Republic derived strength from its strong land army and started expanding out of modern-day Rome, Italy, after the legend of Romulus and Remus established the city of Rome. Both civilizations expanded and they were on a crash course, with everything boiling over as to who would win control over the territory of Sicily. Because of Sicily alone, the two powers fought a bitter 23 year war: the First Punic War (264 BC - 241 BC). The First Punic War ended badly for Carthage, where the Carthaginians were expelled from Sicily and had to pay large reparations to Rome; Sicily became Rome’s first overseas territory and to add insult to injury, Rome annexed the Carthaginian territories of Sardinia and Corsica not long after the end of the war. Hannibal’s father, Hamilcar Barca, was the leading commander of the Carthaginian Army in Sicily and this foreshadowed what was to come.

Hannibal was born in Carthage (247 BC), along with brothers Hasdrubal Barca, Mago Barca, and three sisters, as the oldest son of Hamilcar Barca and his wife. The Barcid family was a wealthy, upper-class, and aristocratic family that had immense military and political influence within Carthaginian society. After the First Punic War and the loss of Sicily, Hamilcar Barca and his forces started focusing on the contested piece of land that Carthage already had a foothold in: Hispania (modern-day Spain). Hamilcar led a push to expand territorially in Hispania to recover lost resources from the war and build a new base of wealth and military strength. He would often take young Hannibal on his campaigns in Hispania, after Hannibal begged to go. Hamilcar made young Hannibal promise and swear an oath at age 9 to “never be a friend of Rome.” Hamilcar unfortunately drowned in battle when Hannibal was 18 years old. Hannibal’s brother-in-law Hasdrubal the Fair subsequently took control of the forces in Hispania and Hannibal served as an officer under Hasdrubal the Fair, further developing his military acumen and war experience. When Hasdrubal the Fair was assassinated, Hannibal took control of the army as commander-in-chief at age 26 (221 BC) after years of training under his father and brother-in-law in the art of war. What happened next was nothing short of legendary.

The Second Punic War (218 BC - 201 BC)


Hannibal’s journey and 15 years of surviving off the land in the Roman heartland.

Crossing the Alps

Hannibal took command of the Iberian forces (Iberia is the Greek name for modern-day Spain) and set off immediately to work. He spent two years consolidating his holdings in Hispania and achieved fantastic results as a first-time general, consolidating all the territory south of the Ebro River under Carthaginian control. Rome saw Hannibal’s rise and feared Carthaginian influence in Hispania; therefore Rome made an alliance with the pro-Roman city of Seguntum, which was south of the Ebro River. Hannibal, for better or for worse, decided to lay siege on Seguntum for eight months and brought the city to its knees. He lost many troops, resources, and was personally injured in the eight month siege, but was relentless in his objectives. This gives an idea of who Hannibal was as a person at his core: he relentlessly pursued his objectives. Rome sent a delegation to Carthage headed by Fabius Maximus demanding answers. When the two sides could not agree on what happened at Seguntum and get to a resolution, the Roman Republic declared war on Carthage and this launched the Second Punic War.

With Seguntum locked-in as his base of operations in late 219 BC, Hannibal spent a significant amount of time in the winter of 219 BC organizing his troops and resources. He had an eclectic set of troops, with infantry made up of Libyan/African, Iberian troops and cavalry made up of Numidian troops (Numidia is modern day Algeria and Tunisia). Hannibal’s goal was always to attack Rome directly and turn her allies against her, a goal he had already mapped out in his mind for years. Many in the Roman political and military establishment believed the war was going to be fought through sea battles, in Iberia/Hispania, or through a land invasion of Carthage. Hannibal had other plans. He devised one of the most audacious plans in human history: crossing over the Alps (the highest and most treacherous mountain range in Europe) directly into Italy to wage war on the Roman heartland. This was unfathomable to many in Rome because the Alps were thought to provide the ultimate protection and the idea that they could be crossed seemed impossible. But not to Hannibal, who said the iconic quote that expressed total self-confidence and determination at the prospect of crossing the Alps: “I shall either find a way or make one!” In spring of 218 BC, Hannibal set off from Seguntum with ~94K infantry, ~12K cavalry, and famously 37 African war elephants to cross the Alps and attack Rome directly. A large portion of his troops were garrisoned in Hispania to protect territory, so only around ~50K infantry, ~9K cavalry, and the 37 African war elephants actually made the treacherous journey of crossing the Alps. The journey was formidable and extremely dangerous. Hannibal and his troops had to deal with multiple existential threats all at the same time: hostility from local, mountainous Gallic tribes who engaged in hit-and-run attacks (they needed to be subdued), narrow paths that ascended into mountains that were slippery with ice and snow, hunger and exhaustion, and the rough weather that lead to frost bites and hypothermia. Crossing the Alps took two weeks and by the time Hannibal and his troops made it into the Po Valley, only ~25K infantry, ~6K cavalry, and only some of the 37 African war elephants survived the journey. It’s one of the biggest mysteries as to which route Hannibal took to cross the Alps, with much debate among historical scholars. But once Hannibal was in the Po Valley, he was able to pick up Gallic insurgents who resented Rome and incorporate them into his army, bolstering his depleted army by the thousands. Having survived such harsh trials and tribulations, Hannibal’s savant-like military genius was about to be unleashed. I think it goes even beyond that: I think the experience of the Alps hardened Hannibal et al. to the point where they were no longer afraid of anything, and that made them a vicious fighting force that was about to wreak havoc in the Roman heartland.


Hannibal Crossing the Alps on an Elephant - Nicolas Poussin (1625)

The Battles of The Second Punic War

Once the Romans learned Hannibal was in the Po Valley with thousands of troops, it was a total SURPRISE for the system. This wasn’t supposed to happen and this was kind of like a glitch in the matrix. The war was believed by many that it would be fought in Hispania and Carthage, but the Romans had to quickly recalibrate and redirect forces to tackle the immediate threat at the door. The way that the Roman Republic worked politically was that two consuls would be elected annually who acted like co-Presidents, and they had authority and military command in times of war. The consul Publius Scipio (father of Scipio Africanus) quickly made his way back to Italy after landing near Marseille, dislodging him from what was supposed to be his offensive campaign in Hispania. Then the consul Sempronius’ planned invasion of Carthage was called off by the Senate. Forces were directed to northern Italy where the goal was to subdue Hannibal by any means necessary. No one could have predicted what happened next as Hannibal’s military genius became evident to all. He grew from a small threat into an existential threat:

  • Battle of Ticinus (218 BC): Hannibal WINS a small cavalry contest where the consul Publius Scipio was wounded severely.

  • Battle of Trebia (218 BC): Hannibal WINS a major battle by luring the combined consular armies of Publius Scipio and Sempronius (~40K soldiers) across an icy river and then unleashed an ambush in the form of a flank maneuver, destroying the combined consular forces. He hid ~2000 elite light infantry soldiers in a dry river bed and unleashed them at exactly the right moment for a total envelopment of the Roman rear guard. Sempronius escapes and the Romans are STUNNED by their loss, but are still not panicking yet.

  • Battle of Lake Trasimene (217 BC): Hannibal WINS a battle when he sets an ambush in foggy conditions along the shores of Lake Trasimene. Hannibal knew that the new consul Gaius Flaminius was an over-confident, arrogant person looking for a big win. To provoke him, Hannibal deliberately ravaged the Etruscan countryside and pillaged/plundered along the way, provoking Flaminius’ anger. Flaminius chased after Hannibal with just his consular army, not waiting for the other consular army. As his troops marched along the narrow shores of Lake Trasimene on a foggy day, Hannibal’s army, hidden in the hills, suddenly attacked from the front, the flanks, and the rear. With Hannibal’s army to one side and Lake Trasimene to the other, ~15K Roman soldiers are trapped and they all perish, including the consul Flaminius himself! This loss SHOCKS Rome and they elect Fabius Maximus as dictator, whose doctrine of the Fabian Strategy (avoiding direct battles with Hannibal and embracing guerilla warfare instead) shapes the rest of the war.

  • Battle of Cannae (216 BC): Hannibal delivers his magnum opus and WINS a monumental battle at Cannae, destroying around ~60K soldiers in one day. To set the context of Cannae, we have to understand what led up to it. In the one year between 217 BC and 216 BC, Hannibal and Fabius Maximus played cat-and-mouse, with Hannibal going on the offensive to plunder and torch the richest parts of Italian farmland: Apulia and Ager Falernus. These were Roman allies and Hannibal’s goal was to: 1) instill doubt in allies’ minds that Rome was going to protect them and 2) draw Fabius Maximus into a direct battle. During this cat-and-mouse game, Hannibal et al. brilliantly escape from a mountain valley death zone by tying torches to the head of cattle, simulating moving troops in the dead of night. The Roman soldiers lunge from their position at the mountain pass towards the lights to later find out they were tricked, which allowed Hannibal and his troops to escape through the mountain pass and escape certain death.

    Being outmaneuvered like this was disastrous for Fabius Maximus, who people thought was a weak and ineffective leader. Resentment had been building that the Fabian Strategy was not strong leadership and not the way to conduct a war as Rome watched Hannibal pillage and plunder. The next year in 216 BC, Varro and Paullus became consuls and they decided to take a more direct approach, just like their contemporaries who failed at Trebia and Tresimene. When Hannibal captured the grain supply depot at Cannae, this sent Rome over the edge because he now cut off an important part of the Roman grain supply chain. Paullus and Varro raised eight legions and ~80K troops, marching towards Hannibal for direct battle. Hannibal had ~50K troops and infantry and decided to employ the double envelopment strategy. He lined up his troops in a crescent shape, purposefully allowing his weaker troops to be in the center as his better troops and cavalry formed the flanks. As the Romans pressed the center, they became outflanked on both sides and were totally enveloped in a circle. It was a disaster, with ~60K soldiers killed and the consul Paullus himself killed all in one day. Multiple important people were killed, including ⅓ of the sitting Roman Senate members who were serving their military duties. By the end of Cannae, 20-25% of fighting-aged men in Rome had been killed. The double envelopment strategy used by Hannibal at Cannae was in many ways revolutionary. It’s still studied today at the top military schools and it has been employed by many subsequent armies, including Napoleon’s Grand Armée during the Napoleonic Wars and the U.S. Army during the Gulf Wars.


Hannibal’s double envelopment strategy that led to the Roman Army’s total destruction at the Battle of Cannae.

15 Years in Enemy Territory

After Cannae, the war had been going on for around two years and Hannibal at that point was 31 years old. Many thought that at this point, Hannibal should have mounted a direct assault on the city of Rome itself. But he did not do so and it’s a famous mystery as to why he did not do it. Perhaps he did not have enough siege equipment or fresh troops to mount a direct assault on a city that was heavily fortified. Or perhaps an assault would have moved him and his troops far away from their precious supply lines. Hannibal was told by one of his commanders: “You know how to win a victory, Hannibal; you do not know how to use it.” Hannibal assumed that by winning battle after battle, the Roman allies in Italy would turn on Rome and that Rome would sue for peace. This was a miscalculation. He did convert some allies, but this period of stasis allowed Rome to raise new legions, build new alliances, and reinforce old alliances. He assumed that the Roman people would quit and sue for peace after losing battle after battle, but they didn’t and dug in even stronger. By employing the Fabian Strategy, Rome was able to gradually restrict Hannibal’s supply lines and dwindle his number of troops. Hannibal had counted on reinforcements and supplies from Carthage, but it never came. A faction of the Carthaginian political elite viewed Hannibal and the Barcid family as too powerful and so they PURPOSEFULLY delayed and then denied sending reinforcements and supplies to Hannibal in Italy! This goes to show an ugly side of human nature that even within a team, they are political games played and jockeying for position, control, and power. Hannibal had also counted on his brother Hasdrubal Barca to reinforce him in Italy; however Hasdrubal and his forces did not time it right and after crossing the Alps, Hasdrubal and his troops were defeated at the Battle of Mataurus (207 BC). In total, Hannibal and his thinning soldiers were in Italy for a total of 15 years living off the land. Most AMAZINGLY however, when they did engage in battle, Hannibal and his forces always showed extreme determination and did not lose one single battle. Some results were undetermined or ties, but under extreme circumstances, Hannibal et al. produced some amazing results. A summarization of the major results Hannibal achieved in Italy after the Battle of Cannae:

  • Silarus (212 BC): Hannibal WINS a battle and destroys a ~16K Roman force under Centenius.
  • First Herdonia (212 BC): Hannibal WINS a battle by setting an ambush on the poorly trained soldiers of the praetor Fulvius Flaccus, killing ~16K out of a total of ~18K soldiers.
  • Capture of Tarentum (212 BC): Hannibal takes a major southern port at night with help from conspirators inside the city, though the Roman garrison holds the citadel.
  • Second Herdonia (210 BC): Hannibal WINS a battle by surprising the proconsul Fulvius. Fulvius is killed, his army destroyed, and the city of Herdonia burned down.
  • Caulonia (209 BC): Hannibal relieves the besieged town and forces a Roman army of about 8,000 to surrender.
  • Trap in Venusia (208 BC): Hannibal pulls off an ambush and the sitting Roman consuls Marcellus and Titus Quinctius are both killed in one day. Marcellus had been harassing Hannibal and his troops for years in the form of hit-and-run attacks. This ambush leaves the Roman military command leaderless and from the top, and is one of Hannibal’s most daring and most underrated maneuvers.
